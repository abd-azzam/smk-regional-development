## PART A — THE MAP

### A.1 The shape of the field (and its main problem)

The single most important structural fact for your thesis: **the two national literatures are not in conversation with each other, and the direct comparative literature is thin and largely poor.**

This is not a minor observation. It shapes what you can and cannot cite, and it defines where your contribution sits.

**What the direct comparative work actually looks like.** Searching specifically for Malaysia–Indonesia higher education comparisons returns:

- A ranking-based descriptive comparison (Suyantiningsih et al., *EduLearn* 2023) that observes Malaysian institutions outperform Indonesian ones on QS metrics and attributes this partly to weaker Indonesian university–industry linkage. Its method is descriptive; its analytical purchase is limited.
- A cluster of 2025 articles in *International Journal of Education, Information Technology, and Others* comparing the two systems via "institutional approach" and "legal-formal approach." These are library-research pieces in a low-visibility venue.
- Character-education and history-curriculum comparisons (Mahanani et al., *IJI* 2022; various) — school-level, not higher education, not STEM.
- The Head Foundation's three-country paper on equity, access and quality (Indonesia, Malaysia, Vietnam, 2016), which is more substantial but is about basic-education equity.

None of these do what you are doing. **There is no serious comparative political-economy account of Malaysian and Indonesian higher education organised around supply-side versus demand-side alignment, and there is certainly none focused on STEM.** That is your gap, and it is a real one.

**Why the two literatures diverged.** Each national field organised itself around its own dominant political problem:

| | Malaysia | Indonesia |
|---|---|---|
| **Central question the field asks** | Who gets in, and on what terms? | Why is quality so bad despite reform? |
| **Dominant explanatory variable** | Ethnicity, NEP, constitutional preference | Predatory elites, patronage, weak governance |
| **Characteristic method** | Policy-document analysis, legislative history, enrolment statistics | Political economy, elite/actor analysis, critical policy studies |
| **Characteristic venue** | *Higher Education Policy*, ISEAS, USM/IPPTN outputs, Malaysian journals | *Journal of Contemporary Asia*, *Comparative Education*, RISE, Lowy |
| **What it tends to ignore** | Whether expansion produced usable capability | Ethnic and regional stratification of access |

The practical consequence: **the Malaysian literature gives you excellent material on the supply-side machinery (quotas, planning, legislation) but is comparatively weak on demand-side outcomes; the Indonesian literature gives you a much sharper account of *why* demand-side reform fails but is thinner on the legislative-institutional detail you have been working from.** You will be doing more original construction on the Indonesian side.

### A.2 The seven clusters

Everything below sorts into seven bodies of work. The clusters are mine, not the field's — the field has not organised itself this way, which is part of the point.

**Cluster 1 — Theory: skill formation, developmental states, human capital.**
This is where your supply/demand framework has to be anchored, and it is the cluster you appear least invested in so far. The Ashton–Green tradition is the natural home for "demand-side alignment" as an analytical category, because it was built precisely to explain why some late-developing states coordinated education output with industrial strategy and others did not. Doner–Ritchie–Slater's systemic-vulnerability argument gives you a *causal* story for why Malaysia and Indonesia both fall short of the Northeast Asian cases — and, importantly, why they fall short differently.

**Cluster 2 — Malaysia: the foundational/historical layer (pre-2000).**
Your Rudner–Selvaratnam–Lee core sits here. This literature is small, hard to find online, and mostly predates the web. Web search on journal names outperforms Consensus and Scholar Gateway here — which matches what you have already found.

**Cluster 3 — Malaysia: ethnicity, access, and affirmative action.**
Large, contentious, and dominated by Lee Hwok Aun. This is where the "demand-side shift is real but partial" nuance you identified gets its evidence: the quota structure did not disappear, it migrated into matriculation. Note the 81.9% Bumiputera share of public-university enrolment figure that recurs across 2023–2026 sources — that number is the single most useful piece of evidence for your "partial shift" argument, and it is recent enough to be a genuine finding rather than a restatement.

**Cluster 4 — Malaysia: 60:40 and the STEM pipeline.**
Enormous in volume, weak in quality. Dozens of papers restate the same three facts (policy dates to 1967, target never met, students find science hard) in low-tier journals. **Be selective and be honest with your supervisor about the quality distribution here.** The genuinely useful items are the ethnographic and critical-policy pieces, plus the Academy of Sciences Malaysia and Khazanah Research Institute grey literature, which has better data than most of the journal articles.

**Cluster 5 — Indonesia: New Order historiography and higher education under authoritarianism.**
This is where your supervisor's interest lives. The key insight from this cluster: New Order higher education policy was *primarily a control problem, not a manpower problem*. NKK/BKK (1978) is the emblematic case — Daoed Joesoef's own justification was technocratic ("fill the techno-structure"), but the function was depoliticisation. That gap between technocratic rhetoric and control function is the Indonesian analogue of your "policy rhetoric ≠ structural change" principle, and it strengthens the comparison considerably.

**Cluster 6 — Indonesia: post-Reformasi political economy and knowledge production.**
Two overlapping schools. Rosser's (predatory elites, institutional governance) explains why reform fails. Rakhmani and Sakhiyya's (cultural political economy, knowledge imaginaries) explains what the reforms *did* produce — a metrics-driven, marketised research system. For a STEM argument, the second school matters more than it might appear: research capacity is the demand-side test that Indonesian higher education most visibly fails.

**Cluster 7 — Link and Match, MBKM, and the vocational track.**
Critical for you. The literature explicitly frames MBKM (2020–) as the descendant of Wardiman's Link and Match — that connection is made openly in the Indonesian literature, not something you have to argue for. This gives your thesis a live contemporary endpoint rather than one that stops in 1998. The Newhouse–Suryadarma vocational-returns work is the strongest empirical evidence in either country's literature that supply-side vocational expansion without demand-side coordination produces poor labour-market returns.

### A.3 Four things the map tells you about your argument

1. **Your supply/demand frame is not standard in either literature.** Nobody in the Malaysian or Indonesian higher-education literature uses it as their organising axis. This is an opportunity, but it means you must import the frame explicitly from Cluster 1 and defend it, rather than citing a national-literature source that already uses it.

2. **The STEM angle narrows your evidence base sharply.** Malaysia has a named, dated, measurable STEM target (60:40, 1967–present, never met). Indonesia has no equivalent — there is no independent national STEM curriculum, and STEM policy has been integrated into science/maths teaching rather than set as a structural target. **This asymmetry is itself a finding, and it maps directly onto your supply/demand argument**: Malaysia set an explicit output target (a supply-side instrument) and failed to hit it for 57 years; Indonesia never set one and produces roughly 18–19% STEM graduates against Malaysia's ~37%. Do not treat the asymmetry as a problem to be smoothed over.

3. **The comparison is not symmetrical in the sources' quality.** Indonesian higher education has attracted better critical scholarship (Rosser, Rakhmani, Welch) than Malaysian higher education has in the same period. Malaysia has better *legislative and statistical* documentation. Play to each.

4. **You will have to build the STEM-specific comparison largely yourself from statistical sources.** No one has done it. Cluster 8 in the list below is your data infrastructure for that.

### A.4 What is genuinely missing (checked, not assumed)

- No comparative Malaysia–Indonesia STEM higher-education study of any analytical seriousness.
- No study connecting Malaysia's 60:40 policy to Indonesia's Link and Match as parallel demand-alignment attempts. This is, as far as I can tell, an unmade comparison — and it is the natural spine of your thesis.
- Little work on Indonesian STEM *higher* education policy specifically; the Indonesian STEM literature is overwhelmingly about school-level pedagogy and teacher perceptions.
- The Mahathir Report (1979) remains, on this evidence, unavailable as a free full PDF. Nothing in today's searching changed that.

  → **The single most important theoretical text for your frame.** Chapter 2 ("The Developmental State and the Education and Training System") and Chapter 7 ("Is There a 'Four Tigers' Model of Skill Formation?") give you the vocabulary for demand-side alignment as a state capacity, not a policy statement. Chapter 8 ("On the Diffusion of the Model") is where you can locate Malaysia and Indonesia as non-adopters or partial adopters.

- **[P] Ashton, D., Green, F., Sung, J., & James, D. (2002).** "The Evolution of Education and Training Strategies in Singapore, Taiwan and S. Korea: A development model of skill formation." *Journal of Education and Work* (venue inferred from citation context; verify).
  → Explicitly challenges the World Bank's account of the state's role in skill formation. Useful if you want to position your argument against human-capital orthodoxy.

- **[V] Green, A.** *Education and State Formation: Europe, East Asia and the USA*, 2nd ed. Palgrave Macmillan (Education, Economy and Society series). Second edition adds the East Asian developmental-state material.
  → Green's argument that citizen-formation often *precedes* and enables economic function is a useful corrective if your Malaysian chapter risks reading the NEP purely as failed manpower planning. The NEP was doing state formation as much as skill formation.

- **[V] Doner, R., Ritchie, B., & Slater, D. (2005).** "Systemic Vulnerability and the Origins of Developmental States: Northeast and Southeast Asia in Comparative Perspective." *International Organization.*
  → **Your causal engine for the Southeast Asian shortfall.** Argues developmental states emerge from a conjunction of pressures (resource constraint, security threat, elite coalition risk) that Malaysia and Indonesia lacked. Explains why both stayed supply-side longer than Korea or Taiwan, and why resource endowment mattered.

- **[V] Booth, A. (1999).** "Initial Conditions and Miraculous Growth: Why Is South East Asia Different from Taiwan and South Korea?" *World Development*, 27(2), 301–321.
  → The colonial-legacy version of the same puzzle. Pairs well with Doner et al.

  → **Very recent and directly relevant.** Argues higher education mattered for SEA development earlier than standard accounts allow. Chase the underlying working paper — the column is a summary, not the source.

- **[R] Woo-Cumings, M. (ed.) (1999).** *The Developmental State.* Cornell University Press. Includes Chang's "The Economic Theory of the Developmental State."
  → Standard reference. Verify chapter details before citing.

### Cluster 3 — Malaysia: ethnicity, access, affirmative action

- **[V] Lee Hwok Aun.** *Affirmative Action in Malaysia and South Africa.* Routledge (ISBN 9781138080072).
  → Chapters 4, 6, and 7 cover Malaysian affirmative-action pillars, the empirical/analytical framework, and outcomes. Note his structural observation that almost all Malaysian universities were established *after* the NEP — a "tabula rasa" that made centralised quota administration far easier than in South Africa. That institutional-design point is directly useful to a supply-side argument.

- **[V] Lee Hwok Aun.** "Malaysia's New Economic Policy and Affirmative Action: A Remedy in Need of a Rethink." Springer reference-work chapter (DOI 10.1007/978-981-33-4016-9_40-1).
  → **Key for your "partial shift" nuance.** Documents that Bumiputera-exclusive pre-university programmes introduced a 10% non-Bumiputera quota in 2001–2002, but that centralised admissions maintained preferences *increasingly through the pre-university channel* rather than at degree entry. This is the mechanism by which the 2002 "abolition" of quotas was not an abolition.

- **[P] Lee Hwok Aun (2012).** "Affirmative Action in Malaysia: Education and Employment Outcomes since the 1990s." Likely *Journal of Contemporary Asia*, 42(2), 230–254 — **the volume/page data came from a reference list and the journal name was garbled in the source; verify.**
  → Contains the finding that graduates of public tertiary institutions (from which most Bumiputera graduate) show relatively greater difficulty in labour-market engagement. **That is a demand-side outcome measure attached to a supply-side instrument — precisely your argument's hinge.**

- **[P] Sua, T. Y., & Santhiram, R. (2017).** Cited for the 55:30:10 (bumiputera : Chinese : Indian) admissions quota structure. Verify the work.

- **[V] Various (2023–2026) on the 81.9% figure.** Free Malaysia Today (Jan 2024, interview with Lee Hwok Aun); Malay Mail (May 2024, Rozilini Mary Fernandez-Chung); East Asia Forum (May 2026).
  → The 81.9% Bumiputera share of public-university enrolment, and the 90:10 matriculation quota, are consistently reported across these. The East Asia Forum piece adds that in 2022 only 13% of total Malaysian income inequality was attributable to between-ethnic-group differences. **Journalism, not scholarship — use for the number, then find the DOSM/MOHE primary source.**

- **[V] Impact of ethnic-quota system on Chinese and Indian students' higher education experience in Malaysia.** University of Edinburgh thesis (era.ed.ac.uk, deposited Dec 2025). Postcolonial theoretical framing; 38 undergraduate participants across Chemical Engineering and Education programmes at five universities. **Author name not captured in the search result — retrieve from the repository record.**
  → Note the Chemical Engineering sample: this is one of very few qualitative studies connecting ethnic quota mechanics to a STEM discipline specifically.

- **[V] Ibrahim Ali.** "Affirmative Action in Higher Education: The Malaysian Experience." ERIC EJ1207798.
  → Useful on the MARA/ITM institutional history: the First Malaysia Plan manpower survey showed professional-level shortage concentrated among bumiputera, who were mostly enrolled in arts and humanities. **This is the documentary origin of the science-stream push and the 60:40 policy in the same breath as the ethnic-redistribution rationale.** Strong evidence that the two logics were entangled from the start rather than sequential.

### Cluster 4 — Malaysia: the 60:40 policy and the STEM pipeline

> [!warning] Quality caution
> This cluster contains the weakest scholarship on this list. A large volume of papers in HRMARS, GA Excellence, KW Publications and similar venues restate the same facts. I have listed the more useful items and flagged the rest as a body rather than individually. Cite the grey literature and the critical pieces; use the journal articles sparingly and knowingly.

**The policy facts, consistently reported across sources:**
- 60:40 was recommended by the Higher Education Planning Committee in **1967**, implemented **1970**. Restated in the 1999 National Education Policy, National Science and Technology Policy II (2000), and the Education Development Plan (2001); reframed as STEM:non-STEM in MEB 2013–2025.
- Never met in 57+ years. Reported peak 48.15% (2012); trough figures of 40.95% (2021) and 41.84% (2019); recovery to 45.73% (2023) and **50.83% (2024)**.
- **[V]** These figures recur across the Academy of Sciences Malaysia (ASM 2018), Ministry of Education annual reports, Bernama, and Khazanah Research Institute outputs.

**Worth actually reading:**

- **[V] "Instances of dis/juncture: STEM education and young people's aspirations for development in the Malaysian *luar bandar*."** *Compare: A Journal of Comparative and International Education* (Taylor & Francis, DOI 10.1080/03057925.2023.2254210). **Author not captured — retrieve.**
  → **The best piece in this cluster.** Ethnographic. Explicitly frames Malaysia as a postcolonial developmental state applying a "science for development" model (drawing on Drori 1998 and Embong 2008) and argues the 60:40 policy "funnels young people in accordance with a logic of human capital for national economic development." **This is a supply-side-instrument critique written by someone who has watched it operate on actual students.** Directly citable for your frame.

- **[V] Khazanah Research Institute (2025).** "Strengthening STEM foundations in primary education to reach the 60:40 goal."
- **[V] Khazanah Research Institute (2025).** "Underemployment and Overqualification among Fresh Graduates."
  → KRI grey literature is better-evidenced than most of the journal literature here. Uses MOHE Graduate Tracer Study data.

- **[P] Zainudin, S., Halim, L., & Iksan, Z. (2015).** The most-cited source in this cluster for 60:40 policy history. **Verify venue.**
- **[P] Shahali, E. H. M., et al. (2017).** Frequently cited on MEB STEM targets. **Verify.**
- **[P] Chua & Choong (2019)**; **[P] Idris et al. (2023a, 2023b)** — the enrolment-trend figures above trace back to these. Chase them for the primary data rather than citing the papers that cite them.

- **[V] Academy of Sciences Malaysia (2018).** Reported the 48.15% (2012) → 45.74% (2017) decline. **Locate the actual ASM report** — it is cited constantly and read rarely.

- **[V] "An insight into primary science education in Malaysia."** White Rose eprints (Leeds/Sheffield/York repository), eprint 208367.
  → Reviews curriculum development, assessment, language of instruction (relevant to your PPSMI material), and pandemic effects. Better-sourced than most.

- **[V] Malay Mail / Yahoo News (May 2024).** Reporting from an engineering-shortage forum: ~5% of 60,000 STEM-major university students change course annually; 43% of engineering graduates and 45% of technology graduates do not work in their field of study.
  → **Journalism, but these are the most useful demand-side mismatch figures I found for Malaysian STEM specifically.** Trace to the original presentation or MOHE data before citing.

### Cluster 5 — Indonesia: New Order historiography and higher education under authoritarianism

- **[V] Human Rights Watch (1998).** *Academic Freedom in Indonesia: Dismantling Soeharto-Era Barriers.* (Available via HRW legacy site and Refworld.)
  → Chapter III ("The Soeharto Legacy on Campus: A Historical Overview") and Chapter VIII ("The Ban on Student Political Activity and Expression") are the most detailed English-language account of NKK/BKK's institutional mechanics: rectors made accountable to military and civilian authorities, campus administrations turned into monitoring institutions. **Note HRW's own framing: the New Order "drew heavily on a range of academic experts, most notably economists, but also engineers, planners" — the technocratic use of universities alongside their political neutralisation.**

- **[V] Aspinall, E. (1993).** *Student Dissent in Indonesia in the 1980s.* Centre of Southeast Asian Studies Monograph, Monash University.
  → Cited in HRW. The standard account of the student-movement side.

- **[V] ANU thesis (author not captured; openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au).** Contains the crucial detail that **Daoed Joesoef's own defence of NKK/BKK was that students needed to concentrate on studies in order to *"mengisi teknostruktur"* — "fill the techno-structure."** Cites Joesoef (1984, p. 70), CSIS (1980), van Dijk (1980, pp. 129–30), and a 1979 ITB student publication, *Misteri dan Tragedi di Balik NKK*.
  → **This is possibly the most useful single find for your Indonesian chapter.** The regime articulated campus depoliticisation in explicitly human-capital-for-industry language. It is the purest available example of your "policy rhetoric ≠ structural change" principle: demand-side vocabulary deployed to justify a control measure. **Retrieve this thesis and identify the author.**

- **[V] "Campus on Fire: Indonesian Universities During the Political Turmoil of 1950s–1960s."** *Archipel* (journals.openedition.org/archipel/612, 2018). **Author not captured — retrieve.**
  → Archive- and interview-based study of the 1965–66 purge's effect on universities across three dimensions: human resources, organisation, and academic activity. **Essential if you want to argue that the New Order's supply-side orientation was partly a *capacity* consequence of having destroyed a generation of academic staff, not only an ideological choice.**

- **[V] Cummings, W. K., & Kasenda, S. (1989).** "The origin of modern Indonesian higher education." In *From Dependence to Autonomy*, pp. 143–166.
  → The standard historical-origins chapter. **Verify the full edited-volume details.**

- **[V] "Indoctrination in Higher Education: Guided Democracy Politics in Campus Environments, 1961–1965."** Springer chapter (DOI 10.1007/978-981-99-1995-6_12). **Author not captured.**
  → Covers Sukarno's PTIP and **UUPT No. 22 of 1961** — the pre-New Order higher education law. If you want the legislative baseline against which New Order policy is measured, this is it. Cites Maxwell (2001).

- **[P] Nugroho, H. (2005).** "The political economy of higher education: the university as an arena..." — seen in a Springer reference list, title truncated. **Verify.**
- **[P] Watson, C. (1986).** "Higher education in Indonesia." *Circle*, 14(41), 39–44.
- **[P] Tilaar, H. A. R. (2012)** and **[P] Soedijarto (2009)** — cited in the RISE literature as representatives of the Indonesian "progressive" nation-building tradition in education. Relevant if you want the domestic intellectual counter-current to technocratic reform.

  → Historical-method study of New Order higher education policy using Kuntowijoyo's framework. Useful for Indonesian-language secondary sourcing and for its own bibliography (references a 2016 article on student movements and NKK/BKK 1978–1983, and Suwirta 2018 on the press).

### Cluster 6 — Indonesia: post-Reformasi political economy and knowledge production

**The Rosser school — why reform fails:**

- **[V] Rosser, A. (2016).** "Neo-liberalism and the political economy of higher education policy in Indonesia." *Comparative Education*, 52(2), 109–135.
  → **Foundational.** Argues neoliberal HE reform met resistance from predatory political, military and bureaucratic elements *and* from popular forces, producing continuity with **the centralist, predatory system established under the New Order.** That continuity claim is exactly the bridge your thesis needs between the New Order chapter and the contemporary one.

- **[V] Rosser, A. (2023).** "Higher Education in Indonesia: The Political Economy of Institution-Level Governance." *Journal of Contemporary Asia*, 53(1). DOI 10.1080/00472336.2021.2010120.
  → Three-institution case study. Argues governance failure reflects predatory dominance and the marginalisation of actors supporting quality — whether understood in neoliberal or idealist terms. Change happens only when reformists capture an institution top-down or leverage external actors.

- **[V] Rosser, A. (2019).** "Big Ambitions, Mediocre Results: Politics, Power and the Quest for World-Class Universities in Indonesia." In Jarvis, D., & Mok, K. (eds), *Transformations in Higher Education Governance in Asia*. Singapore: Springer, pp. 81–99.
  → On the world-class-university push: the 2006 ten-institution task force, the 2007 expansion to 50 institutions. **Useful as the Indonesian counterpart to Malaysia's MyBrain15/research-university programme — both are prestige-driven supply-side instruments dressed as quality reform.**

- **[V] Rosser, A. (2018).** *Beyond access: Making Indonesia's education system work.* Lowy Institute Analysis.
  → The accessible synthesis: "a high-volume, low-quality enterprise." Good for a framing quotation, though remember it is a think-tank paper.

- **[V] Rosser, A., & King, P.** *The Political Economy of the Learning Crisis in Indonesia.* RISE Working Paper (riseprogramme.org).
  → Argues technocratic and progressive elements have contested predatory orientation with occasional success, but contestation generally settles in favour of predatory elites — so funding increases and best-practice templates will not deliver absent a reconfiguration of power. **The strongest available statement of why Indonesian demand-side reform keeps failing.**

- **[V] Rosser, A., & Fahmi, M. (2018).** "The political economy of teacher management reform in Indonesia." *International Journal of Educational Development.* (Also: World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 7913, 2016, DOI 10.1596/1813-9450-7913.)
- **[V] Rosser, A., & Joshi, A. (2013).** "From User Fees to Fee-Free: The Politics of Realising Universal Free Basic Education in Indonesia." *Journal of Development Studies*, 49(2), 175–189.

**The Rakhmani–Sakhiyya school — what reform produced:**

- **[V] Rakhmani, I., & Sakhiyya, Z. (2024).** "The Cultural Political Economy of Knowledge in Neo-Liberal Indonesia." *Journal of Contemporary Asia.* DOI 10.1080/00472336.2023.2298449.
  → Gramscian. Introduces "knowledge imaginaries" — self-reliance, competitiveness, entrepreneurship — normalised into common sense such that managers can control academic productivity. **For a STEM argument: this explains why Indonesian research output rose without research capability rising correspondingly.**

- **[V] Rakhmani, I., & Sakhiyya, Z. (2024).** "Inequalities and social sciences in neoliberal Indonesia." *International Social Science Journal*, 74, 9–23.

- **[V] "Merdeka Belajar": A Cultural Political Economy of Indonesia's Neoliberalising Nationalist University.** *Masyarakat: Jurnal Sosiologi*, 29(1), article 6 (scholarhub.ui.ac.id/mjs/vol29/iss1/6). **Authorship not confirmed in the search result — likely Rakhmani, but verify.**
  → **Directly on your thesis line.** Argues the marketisation of higher education "discursively and materially reconfigures New Order-style development planning and stylises it into ways that accommodate neoliberal demands." That is a continuity-with-transformation claim about exactly the supply-side-to-demand-side transition you are tracing.

- **[V] Rakhmani, I., Sakhiyya, Z., Ramadhan, A. S., & Agahari, W. (2020).** *Doing Research in Indonesia: Bridging the research gap and improving development policies.* Centre for Innovation Policy and Governance (CIPG) and the Global Development Network.

- **[V] Achwan, R. (2017).** "Production of Social Science in Indonesia: An Incomplete Reform From Above." *Asian Politics & Policy.*
  → Notes Indonesia's ranking at the bottom of Asian developing countries for social science publications, below states with lower HDI and GDP per capita. Also covers the 2005 constitutional amendment requiring 20% of government budget on education. Social-science-focused, but the institutional diagnosis generalises.

- **[V] Siregar, F. (2020).** "Utility over excellence: Doing research in Indonesia." In Kraemer-Mbula, E., Tijssen, R., Wallace, M. L., & McLean, R. (eds), *Transforming Research Excellence: New Ideas from the Global South*, pp. 119–137. African Minds.

- **[V] "Industrial Mindset of Education in Merdeka Belajar Kampus Merdeka (MBKM) Policy."** *Jurnal Kajian Peradaban Islam* (jkpis.com), 2021.
  → States directly: "Link and match was an initial policy in the 1990s while the MBKM program is improved." - **[V] "Preparing the Implementation of Merdeka Belajar – Kampus Merdeka Policy in Higher Education Institutions."** Atlantis Press (ICOSIHESS-20 proceedings), 2021.

- **[V] The contested replication literature.** Mahirda, K., & Wahyuni, H.   → **Cite the disagreement, not one side of it.** The fact that returns to Indonesian vocational education are contested across two decades of studies is itself evidence that the supply expansion was not demand-coordinated.

- **[V] The SMK expansion facts.** 2006 national vocational expansion programme; SMA:SMK ratio target reversed from 70:30 to 30:70 (reported in a *Children and Youth Services Review* article, 2020: "Vocational education in Indonesia: History, development, opportunities, and challenges," DOI via S0190740920301134); earlier targets of 50:50 by 2010 and 70:30 by 2015 (per Mahirda & Wahyuni). Presidential Instruction No. 9/2016 on SMK revitalisation.

- **[V] di Gropello, E. (2013).** "Role of the education and training sector in addressing skill mismatch in Indonesia." In Suryadarma, D., & Jones, G. (eds), *Education in Indonesia*, pp. 236–266. Singapore: ISEAS.

- **[V] IDinsight (2024).** *Evidence review: Implementation of a vocational school transformation program in Indonesia* (SMK-PK). 2,172 schools over two years.
  → Contemporary evaluation evidence on school–industry partnership. Grey literature but methodologically careful.
